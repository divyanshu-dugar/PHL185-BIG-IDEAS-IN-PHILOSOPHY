## Socrates, the Sophists, and the Meaning of Knowledge

## 1. The Sophists: Persuasion Over Truth

Before understanding Socrates, we must understand **the Sophists**, because Socrates defined himself *against* them.

### Who Were the Sophists?

* Professional **teachers and diplomats** in ancient Athens
* Traveled from city to city
* **Charged fees** to teach rhetoric and persuasion
* Taught skills useful for success in politics and public life

### Core Sophist Beliefs

* There is **no absolute truth**
* Truth varies:

  * From culture to culture
  * From time to time
  * From individual to individual
* **Knowledge cannot be clearly distinguished from opinion**

### Their Goal

* Not to discover truth
* But to **win arguments**
* If truth is relative, then persuasion is more important than correctness

> For the Sophists, success meant convincing others — not being right.

---

## 2. Skepticism vs Sophism

* **Sophism**: Uses skepticism about truth to justify persuasion
* **Skepticism**: Questions knowledge but does not necessarily reject truth

Socrates accepts **intellectual humility**, but rejects the Sophists’ conclusion that truth does not matter.

---

## 3. Socrates and Plato

### Socrates

* Did **not write anything**
* Believed philosophy should be lived, not written
* Known only through others’ writings

### Plato

* Student of Socrates
* Deeply influenced by him
* Wrote philosophical works in the form of **dialogues** (like plays)
* In most dialogues, **Socrates is the main character**

---

## 4. Plato’s Dialogues: Three Periods

### 1. Early Dialogues

Examples:

* *Euthyphro*
* *Crito*
* *Apology*

Characteristics:

* Reflect **Socrates’ own views**
* Focus on ethical questions
* Use the **Socratic Method**
* Often end without a final answer

---

### 2. Middle Dialogues

Examples:

* *Meno*
* *Phaedo*
* *Symposium*
* *Republic*

Characteristics:

* Plato begins expressing **his own philosophy**
* Still presented through Socrates
* Introduce deeper metaphysical ideas

---

### 3. Late Dialogues

Example:

* *Laws*

Characteristics:

* Address **problems and limitations** of earlier theories
* More technical and complex
* Socrates’ role is reduced
* Plato increasingly speaks in his own voice

---

## 5. The Socratic Method

### Education as Discovery, Not Transmission

* Socrates believed **knowledge already exists within the soul**
* The role of education is to **draw it out**, not insert it

> Socrates compared himself to a **midwife** — helping ideas be born, not creating them.

### The Role of the Teacher

* Not to lecture
* Not to give answers
* But to help students **discover truth for themselves**

---

## 6. Irony and Questioning

The Socratic Method begins with **irony**:

* Socrates pretends to be ignorant
* He asks others to teach him
* Through questioning, he reveals that they do not truly understand what they claim to know

This exposes **false confidence** and intellectual arrogance.

---

## 7. Dialectic: Progress Through Questioning

Dialectic is the structured conversation Socrates uses to approach truth.

### How Dialectic Works

1. Socrates asks a question (e.g., *What is justice?* *What is piety?*)
2. Someone proposes an answer
3. Socrates asks follow-up questions
4. Contradictions or problems appear
5. The answer is shown to be inadequate

This process:

* Eliminates false answers
* Moves closer to truth
* Shows that **knowing requires examination**

> Progress is made by discovering what we do **not** know.

---

## 8. Socrates vs the Sophists

* Sophists claim to know and teach wisdom
* Socrates shows they **know nothing**
* Sophists seek victory
* Socrates seeks truth

This made Socrates deeply unpopular.

---

## 9. The Oracle of Delphi and Socratic Wisdom

### The Story

* Socrates’ friend **Chaerephon** asked the Oracle of Delphi:

  > “Is anyone wiser than Socrates?”
* The Oracle replied: **No**

### Socrates’ Investigation

* Socrates was confused by the answer
* He questioned politicians, poets, and craftsmen
* He discovered:

  * They believed they were wise
  * But could not justify their knowledge

### Socrates’ Conclusion

> Socrates is wiser only because **he knows that he is not wise**.

True wisdom = **awareness of ignorance**.

---

## 10. Socrates’ View of Virtue (Arete)

### Virtue as Human Excellence

* **Arete** means excellence or virtue
* To live well, humans must achieve excellence in being human

### Virtue Is Knowledge

* Virtue is a form of **wisdom**
* To be good is to know what is good

---

## 11. Argument from the *Meno*

Socrates’ reasoning:

1. Virtue is beneficial
2. Things like wealth and health are beneficial **only if used wisely**
3. Without wisdom, they can cause harm
4. Therefore, virtue must be **a kind of wisdom**

---

## 12. Arete and Techne

Socrates explains virtue using **crafts**:

* Shoemakers
* Metalworkers
* Craftsmen

### Techne

* Greek term meaning **practical knowledge**
* Knowing *how* to do something
* Not abstract theory, but applied understanding

> Knowledge is not just information — it is knowing how to act.

Virtue is a **special kind of techne**:

* Knowledge of how to live well
* Combined with character and judgment

---

## 13. Wrongdoing as Ignorance

From “virtue is knowledge,” Socrates concludes:

* No one knowingly does wrong
* If someone does wrong, it is because they **do not truly know the good**
* People always act according to what they believe is good

> We never intend to do what we believe is evil.

---

## 14. The Craft of Living Well

Living well is itself a **craft**:

* Just as a metalworker understands metal
* A good person understands **the self**

### Know Thyself

* Self-knowledge is essential
* One must work on the self as a craft

From the *Apology*:

> **“The unexamined life is not worth living.”**

The highest task:

* Care for the **psyche**

### Psyche

* Combination of soul and body
* The soul is imperishable
* Moral development matters more than wealth or reputation

---

## 15. The Trial of Socrates (Historical Context)

### Political Background

* Athens lost the **Peloponnesian War** against Sparta
* Resulted in humiliation and instability
* Civil conflict between democrats and oligarchs

### Why Socrates Was Targeted

* He publicly exposed ignorance
* He challenged tradition and authority
* He was seen as a dangerous social critic

### The Charges

* Not worshipping the gods of the state
* Corrupting the youth

Many Athenians believed:

* Questioning traditions weakened Athens
* Socrates made the city vulnerable

This led to his trial.

* Socrates defended himself, but he was judged guilty by a rather close vote.
* He was sentenced to death. He accepted the judgment and refused to flee into exile.
* He was put to death by drinking a cup of poison hemlock.

## Key Thread So Far

* Sophists: persuasion without truth
* Socrates: truth through examination
* Knowledge over opinion
* Virtue as wisdom
* Philosophy as a way of life, not a profession

> Philosophy begins when we stop asking how to win — and start asking how to live.

## Plato: Knowledge, Forms, and the Good

> **Status:** Draft notes based on the continuation of Lecture 3. These build directly on Socrates and the Sophists and will be expanded later.

---

## 1. Plato in Context

* **Plato (427–347 BCE)**
* Student of **Socrates**, teacher of **Aristotle**
* Founded **the Academy (Academus)**, one of the first formal schools of philosophy

Plato’s philosophy grows out of Socrates’ project but tries to **solve problems Socrates left open**, especially:

* Skepticism
* Relativism
* The problem of reliable knowledge

---

## 2. Plato’s Central Aim

Plato wanted to:

* Justify **how knowledge is possible**
* Reject **Sophist relativism** (truth as persuasion)
* Show that **objective truth and goodness exist**
* Establish a rational foundation for a **just state**

Even though our **senses deceive us**, Plato argues that **reason can grasp truth**.

---

## 3. Knowledge vs. Opinion

Plato makes a strict distinction between **knowledge** and **opinion**.

### Knowledge

* Enduring and unchangeable
* Always true
* Backed by **logos** (a rational explanation or account)
* Comes from instruction and understanding
* Cannot be overturned by persuasion or rhetoric

### Opinion

* Changeable
* Can be true or false
* Not supported by logos
* Implanted through persuasion
* Easily manipulated

> The Sophists operate in the realm of opinion. Plato wants philosophy to operate in the realm of knowledge.

---

## 4. The Problem of Skepticism

Plato agrees with the Sophists on one important point:

* **The senses are unreliable**

Examples:

* Mirages
* Hallucinations
* A stick appearing bent in water
* The brain distorting reality

If everything is constantly changing, **how can we have stable knowledge?**

Plato’s answer: **knowledge does not come from sensation, but from reason**.

---

## 5. Why Reason, Not Sensation?

When you see a dog:

* Your senses receive changing images
* But you recognize it as a *dog*

This recognition is not sensory — it is **conceptual**.

You already possess the concept *dog*, and you apply it rationally.

> We do not see with pure sensation; we always see through concepts.

---

## 6. What Is a Form (Idea)?

If A, B, and C are different animals, why do we call them all *dogs*?

Because they share something in common:

* **The Dog Itself**

This shared essence is called a **Form** (or **Idea**).

### Key Points About Forms

* Forms are universal
* Particular things are examples of Forms
* A physical dog resembles the **Form of Dog**
* Particulars are imperfect instances of the Form

> Platonic Ideas and Platonic Forms mean the same thing.

---

## 7. Forms and Concepts

Forms correspond to the **concepts in our minds**, but they are **not created by our minds**.

* Forms exist independently
* Our minds apprehend them

---

## 8. Why Senses Cannot Give Us Knowledge of Forms

Example: **The Square**

* No physical square is perfect
* All drawn squares are imperfect
* Yet we know what a square is

The properties of a square (e.g., four equal sides, perimeter rules) are grasped by **reason**, not senses.

> The Square Itself is apprehended intellectually, not visually.

---

## 9. Platonic Forms Explained

* Forms are **universals**
* Universals allow us to categorize things
* The Form of a thing is what makes it the kind of thing it is

Knowledge is not about changing objects, but about **unchanging Forms**.

---

## 10. Two Levels of Reality

### The Visible World (World of Becoming)

* Material
* Ever-changing
* Known through senses
* Less real

### The Intelligible World (World of Being)

* Immaterial
* Eternal and unchangeable
* Known through reason
* More real

Examples of Forms:

* The Dog Itself
* The Square Itself
* The Just Itself
* The Pious Itself

---

## 11. Particulars and Forms: Participation

Particular things **participate** in Forms.

* A particular dog participates in the Form of Dog
* Particulars resemble Forms
* Particulars depend on Forms for what they are

Plato explains this using **shadows**:

* A shadow resembles an object
* But it is less real
* It depends entirely on the object

Particulars exist between:

* Complete unreality
* Perfect reality (Forms)

---

## 12. Plato’s Epistemology

Knowledge of Forms:

* Is **not learned through the senses**
* Is already present in the soul

### Innate Knowledge

* The soul existed before birth
* The soul encountered the Forms
* Learning is actually **recollection**

> We do not acquire knowledge; we remember it.

---

## 13. Argument for the Existence of Forms

1. Knowledge is enduring, true, rational belief based on instruction
2. We do possess knowledge
3. Sensory objects are changing and unreliable
4. Therefore, knowledge must be about something enduring
5. That enduring reality is the **world of Forms**

---

## 14. Meaning of Words and Universals

Why do we use one word for many individuals?

* John and Jack are particulars
* “Human” applies to many beings

This is because:

* We assume a common **Form of the Human Itself**

Language itself points to the existence of Forms.

---

## 15. Plato’s View of the Soul

Plato draws a sharp distinction between body and soul.

### Soul

* Exists independently of the body
* Is immaterial
* Is immortal
* Knows Forms

### Body

* Material
* Temporary
* Subject to change

The soul’s descent into the body is described as **the Fall**.

---

## 16. The Structure of the Soul

Plato describes the soul using the **Myth of the Charioteer**.

### Three Parts of the Soul

1. **Intellect (Reason)**

   * The charioteer
   * Guides and governs

2. **Passionate Part**

   * Noble horse
   * Supports reason

3. **Desirous Part**

   * Unruly horse
   * Seeks pleasure

Moral conflict arises when desire resists reason.

A good life requires **reason to rule**.

---

## 17. The Form of the Good

The highest Form is the **Form of the Good**.

* Explains why things are the way they are
* Makes knowledge possible
* Makes reality intelligible
* Is the ultimate cause of existence

> To understand anything fully, we must understand why it is good for it to be so.

---

## 18. Plato’s Dialectic

Dialectic is the method of intellectual ascent.

* Moves from sensible objects to Forms
* Moves from lower Forms to higher Forms
* Ends with the Form of the Good

Dialectic is how philosophy reaches ultimate explanation.

---

## 19. The Allegory of the Cave

### Inside the Cave (Sensible World)

* Prisoners see only shadows
* Shadows are mistaken for reality
* Comparable to relying on poets, media, images

### Outside the Cave (Intelligible World)

* Prisoner sees real objects
* Gradually sees higher realities
* Finally sees the sun

The **sun represents the Form of the Good**.

---

## 20. The Return to the Cave

If the freed prisoner returns:

* Others resist the truth
* They prefer familiar illusions
* They may kill the truth-teller

This mirrors:

* Socrates’ fate
* Society’s resistance to philosophy

---

## Key Thread So Far

* Socrates: ethical inquiry and intellectual humility
* Plato: metaphysical foundation for knowledge
* From opinion to knowledge
* From senses to reason
* From shadows to reality

> Philosophy is the difficult ascent from appearance to truth.
